## MTP Lands in llama.cpp, and the Speedup Story Is More Nuanced Than the Celebration Suggests

Multi-Token Prediction support merged into llama.cpp this week, and the local AI community is treating it as an unambiguous win. It mostly is, but the benchmark results from Strix Halo hardware tell a more specific story that matters for anyone thinking about deploying local models in agentic or chat workflows.

The short version: MTP helps decode-bound workloads and hurts prefill-bound ones. On the 27B dense model, multi-turn chat got 26% faster end-to-end, with generation throughput more than doubling. That is a meaningful improvement for the kinds of long-context, back-and-forth sessions that characterize real agent loops. On the 35B MoE model, the picture flips: generation tokens per second improved, but the prefill penalty made total wall time slightly worse across the same workload.

This distinction matters strategically. MoE architectures already process prompts efficiently, so speculative decoding's prefill overhead is harder to absorb. Dense models with slower prefill have more room to trade. If you are choosing between Qwen3.6-27B and 35B-A3B for a local agentic deployment where sessions involve growing context over multiple turns, the 27B now has a stronger practical argument than the raw capability gap might suggest.

The broader point is that llama.cpp MTP support lands at a moment when local inference is becoming genuinely competitive for developer tooling. A 136% generation speedup on consumer hardware is not a research result. It is a workflow change. Developers who have been waiting for local models to feel responsive in coding-agent contexts have a new configuration worth testing.

The merge happened; the benchmark work to understand when it helps and when it does not is just starting.

### Harnesses, Not Models, Determine Output Quality

The generator-evaluator experiment with 12 adversarial iterations to build a marketing site is easy to dismiss as a clever demo. It is more useful as a product principle. The author's key observation, that the harness determines whether you get slop or something distinctive, maps directly to what separates good AI product work from bad. Single-shot generation optimizes for average. Structured iteration with real feedback (in this case, Playwright actually browsing the live site) creates the conditions for creative deviation. The "Terminal Noir" pivot at iteration 4 did not come from a better model. It came from cumulative evaluator pressure penalizing generic AI patterns. This is the architectural insight that matters: constraints plus loops beat prompts alone.

### CTFs, Arxiv, and the Integrity Debt Coming Due

Two stories about AI eroding trust in existing systems landed this week and they rhyme. Frontier models have apparently made open CTF competitions unworkable because teams using AI can solve challenges faster than organizers can design them. Separately, the backlash against Arxiv's proposed one-year ban for papers with hallucinated references revealed that a meaningful number of academic authors have been signing papers they have not read. Both situations expose the same problem: systems built on assumed human effort are fragile when AI can substitute at scale. CTF organizers and journal editors are now in the same position as anyone who built trust infrastructure on the assumption that doing the work was hard.
